# The eight baseline figures — what each one shows and why it matters

Generated by `11_baseline_viz.py`. Colours come from the validated dataviz
reference palette; **each model keeps the same colour in every figure**, so you
can track "the aqua line" across all eight.

All curves and the confusion matrices use **seed 0**. The per-attack heatmap
(fig_b4) averages **all five seeds**, so its numbers match the table in
`BASELINES.md` §6.3 exactly. Seed-to-seed spread is in §6.2.

---

## fig_b1_confusion.png — Confusion matrices

**What a confusion matrix is.** A 2×2 table of what happened. Rows are the
truth (was this window actually anomalous?), columns are the decision (did the
model flag it?). The four cells:

| | not flagged | flagged |
|---|---|---|
| **actually normal** | true negative — correctly ignored | **false positive** — a wasted investigation |
| **actually anomalous** | **false negative** — a missed spoofer | true positive — a catch |

**Why all six use the same alert budget.** A confusion matrix depends entirely
on where you put the cutoff. Set it low and you flag everything (perfect recall,
useless precision); set it high and you flag nothing. To compare models fairly
they must all flag the **same number of windows** — here the top 3.5% by score,
1,025 of 29,292, chosen to match the true anomaly rate. So every matrix has the
same column totals, and the only thing that differs is *which* windows.

**Colour** encodes the row percentage, not the raw count. Raw counts run from
27,585 down to 218; a count ramp would paint one cell black and three white.

**What to read off it.** Random forest catches 353 of 1,035 anomalies (34.1%)
and burns 672 false alarms. The MLP — 51 parameters — catches 355, one more.
That near-identity is the whole point of parameter matching.

## fig_b2_roc.png — ROC curves

**What ROC is.** As you slide the cutoff from "flag nothing" to "flag
everything", you trace a curve of *anomalies caught* against *normal traffic
wrongly flagged*. The diagonal is random guessing. Area under the curve (AUC) is
one number summarising it: 0.5 is worthless, 1.0 is perfect.

**The interpretation worth knowing:** AUC is the probability that a randomly
chosen anomaly scores higher than a randomly chosen normal window. Random
forest's 0.787 means it gets that right about 79 times in 100.

**The dots** mark where each model actually sits at the 3.5% budget. They are
all crammed into the far-left corner — that is the operating regime that matters,
and it is a tiny slice of the curve. **A model can look good across the whole
curve and still be poor where you actually use it.**

## fig_b3_pr.png — Precision–recall curves

**Why this exists alongside ROC.** With 3.5% anomalies, the 96.5% that are
normal dominate the false-positive-rate axis, so ROC is generous. Precision–
recall ignores true negatives entirely and asks the operational question: *of
the alerts I open, how many are real?*

- **Precision** = of the windows flagged, the fraction genuinely anomalous.
- **Recall** = of the real anomalies, the fraction flagged.
- **Average precision (AP)** = area under this curve. Random guessing scores
  the base rate, 0.035 — the dashed line.

**The finding this figure exists to show.** Isolation Forest is 4th on ROC-AUC
but **last here, at 0.133 against logistic regression's 0.283**. Both look
similar on ROC; on the metric that reflects an analyst's day, labelled training
data more than doubles performance. Report both metrics or you will mislead
yourself.

## fig_b4_per_attack.png — Per-attack performance (mean of 5 seeds)

Two panels, deliberately.

**Left — AUC per attack**, threshold-free. Each cell scores that model against
normal traffic *and one attack type only*. Reading down the drift column: every
model is 0.574–0.611, and the highest score belongs to the **zero-parameter
threshold rule**. No classical learner extracts drift signal that one number and
a cutoff does not already have.

**Right — recall per attack at the 3.5% budget.** This is the panel that changes
the story. Random forest's dark AUC of 0.793 sounds respectable; at the actual
operating point it catches **6.9% of dark attacks**. Drift: **2.3%**.

**In practice these models detect speed_lie and jump, and almost nothing else.**
AUC hid that, because AUC averages over thresholds nobody operates at.

## fig_b5_enrichment.png — Does a higher score mean a higher chance of anomaly?

Test windows are sorted by score and cut into 20 bands of 5%. For each band:
what fraction of those windows were actually anomalous? A flat line at the 3.5%
base rate would mean the score carries no information at all.

**Why percentile rank on the x-axis.** The six models emit incompatible
quantities — probabilities, negative isolation path lengths, raw knot values.
Ranking each model's own scores puts all six on one comparable axis.

**Log y-axis** because the range spans 0.4% to 28%; linear would compress the
bottom half to nothing.

**What to read.** Every model rises monotonically — the scores are real. But the
top band tops out near **28%**: even among the highest-scoring 5% of all traffic,
roughly 3 in 4 windows are still normal. That is the hard ceiling on precision,
and it is a property of the features, not of any model.

## fig_b6_budget.png — Performance against the real constraint

Neither ROC nor PR answers the question an operations centre actually asks:
*we can review X% of traffic — what do we get?* This sweeps X from 0.5% to 20%.

- **Left:** anomalies caught. At 3.5%, roughly 34%. To reach 50% you must review
  about 10% of all traffic.
- **Right:** alerts worth opening. Falls steeply — reviewing more traffic always
  means a higher share of duds.

The dashed line marks the 3.5% budget used everywhere else. Use this figure to
justify that choice, and to state honestly what the system would deliver at any
other staffing level.

## fig_b7_importance.png — What the models actually use

**Left: permutation importance (random forest).** Shuffle one feature's values
so it becomes noise, and measure how far AUC falls. A big drop means the model
depended on it. *Permutation* importance rather than the built-in "impurity"
kind, which is known to inflate the apparent importance of features with many
distinct values. Computed on **validation**, so nothing diagnostic touches test.

**Right: logistic regression coefficients.** Because all eight features are on
the same [0, π] scale, the coefficients are directly comparable. Blue pushes the
anomaly score up, red pushes it down.

**Three things to take from it:**

1. `spd_mismatch_max` dominates both models. The speed-versus-position
   contradiction is the backbone of the whole detector.
2. `prev_silence_s` is the random forest's **second** most important feature but
   nearly irrelevant to logistic regression — the forest is using it in
   combination with others in a way a linear model cannot.
3. `crab_valid`, `sog_mean` and `crab_mean` contribute almost nothing. These are
   your **ablation candidates** — the ones to test dropping.

## fig_b8_val_test.png — The validation-to-test drop

Every model scores lower on test than on validation. Normally that means
selection overfit the validation set — but **the threshold rule drops too**, and
it has zero trainable parameters and three candidate configurations. A model
that *cannot* overfit still dropped, so the cause is the splits.

The **middle column** is the diagnostic: test anomalies resampled so their attack
blend matches validation's, normals untouched, averaged over 30 draws. That
recovers 35–39% of the gap. Test carries +9.9 percentage points more drift — the
hardest attack — than validation does.

The remaining ~4 points are simply that the 609 test vessels are harder than the
364 validation vessels.

**Why we are not fixing it.** Every model is scored on the identical test set, so
no comparison is affected, and a harder test set makes a claimed quantum
advantage *harder* to obtain — the conservative direction. It goes in
limitations.
